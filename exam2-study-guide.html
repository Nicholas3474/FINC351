Exam 1 refresher · Sessions 1–9

The tools Exam 2 is built on

CAPM and the index model are built from covariances. If these mechanics are solid, the new material is mostly new interpretation.

You're ready when you can…

Learn

1

Expected return, variance, standard deviation

Two versions. For scenarios, weight by probabilities. For historical data, average and divide squared deviations by n − 1.

ScenariosE(r) = Σ psrs    σ2 = Σ ps[rs − E(r)]2
Historical sampler̄ = 1nΣ rt    σ2 = 1n − 1Σ (rt − r̄)2
TrapSquare decimals, not percents. A 20% σ means variance 0.04, not 400.
2

Covariance and correlation

Covariance tells you whether two returns move together. Correlation rescales it to between −1 and +1.

Cov(ri, rj) = Σ ps[ri,s − E(ri)][rj,s − E(rj)]    ρi,j = Cov(ri, rj)σiσj

Two facts you'll use all the time: Cov(ri, rj) = ρσiσj, and Cov(ri, ri) = σi2.

3

The double summation

Portfolio variance is the sum of every cell of the covariance matrix, each multiplied by both weights. With one asset (w1 = 1), it collapses to σ12.

σp2 = Σi Σj wiwjCov(ri, rj)  ⟹  two assets: w12σ12 + w22σ22 + 2w1w2Cov(r1, r2)

Group the matrix by rows and each row becomes one asset's covariance with the portfolio:

Cov(ri, rp) = Σj wjCov(ri, rj)    σp2 = Σi wiCov(ri, rp)
From the lecture notes“The variance of the portfolio is the weighted average of each asset's covariance with the portfolio.” An asset's risk on its own is σi2. As a member of a portfolio, its risk is Cov(ri, rp). This one idea leads straight to β.
4

CAL, Sharpe ratio, special weights

Combining a risky portfolio T with the risk-free asset traces a straight line. Its slope is T's Sharpe ratio. The tangency portfolio has the steepest CAL.

CAL: E(rP) = rf + E(rT) − rfσTσP    weight in T: y = σP/σT
wi(min) = σj2 − Cov(ri, rj)σi2 + σj2 − 2Cov(ri, rj)
wi(tan) = [E(ri) − rf]σj2 − [E(rj) − rf]Cov(ri, rj)[E(ri) − rf]σj2 + [E(rj) − rf]σi2 − [E(ri) − rf + E(rj) − rf]Cov(ri, rj)

Session 11 · Diversification

Diversification

Why big portfolios care about covariance and not variance, and why one kind of risk never goes away.

You're ready when you can…

Learn

1

Two kinds of risk

Firm-specific risk (also called unique, idiosyncratic or diversifiable) comes from news about one company: a lawsuit, a CEO leaving, a product flop. Systematic risk (market or nondiversifiable risk) comes from economy-wide forces that hit almost every stock at once: recessions, interest rates, inflation.

From the lecture notes“Covariance measures systematic risk, variance measures total risk. The larger the portfolio, the lower its firm-specific risk.” Total risk = systematic + firm-specific.
2

What happens as you add stocks

Take an equally weighted portfolio (w = 1/n). The n × n covariance matrix has n variance cells on the diagonal and n(n − 1) covariance cells off it. Each cell is multiplied by (1/n)(1/n). Group them:

σp2 = 1n·(avg variance) + n − 1n·(avg covariance)

As n grows, the first term goes to zero and the second goes to the average covariance. For big portfolios, covariances are all that matter. When every stock has the same σ and the same pairwise ρ, avg covariance = ρσ2, so the floor is σ√ρ.

TrapPortfolio σ is not the weighted average of the σ's unless ρ = +1. With ρ = +1 there is no diversification benefit at all.
3

Each asset's contribution to portfolio risk

From the double summation, σp2 = Σ wiCov(ri, rp). So asset i contributes wiCov(ri, rp), and its share of portfolio variance is:

sharei = wiCov(ri, rp)σp2   (shares add up to 100%)

A stock's own variance barely matters inside a big portfolio. What matters is how it co-moves with everything else. That's why a high-σ stock can still lower portfolio risk if its covariance with the portfolio is low.

4

The end point is the market

Keep diversifying and you end up holding everything: the market portfolio. It's the only risky portfolio with zero firm-specific risk, so its risk is 100% systematic. In the lecture sketch of σ vs. n, a single stock X sits high, B and A sit lower, and M sits at the bottom. σM is the floor.

Sessions 12–13 · Capital Market Line

The Capital Market Line

If everyone agrees on the inputs, everyone holds the same risky portfolio. That portfolio has to be the market.

You're ready when you can…

Learn

1

From CAL to CML

Under the CAPM assumptions, every investor uses the same expected returns, σ's and correlations and the same risk-free rate. So everyone draws the same efficient frontier and picks the same tangency portfolio. The market is just everyone's holdings added together, so the tangency portfolio is the market portfolio M. The CAL through M is the CML.

CML: E(rp) = rf + E(rM) − rfσMσp

The slope is the market Sharpe ratio, the reward per unit of total risk. No portfolio can beat it: “The only way to get the biggest possible Sharpe ratio is to diversify as much as possible by holding the market portfolio.”

2

Where you sit on the line

Let y be the fraction of wealth in M, with the rest in the risk-free asset.

σp = yσM    E(rp) = rf + y[E(rM) − rf]    βp = y
  • y < 1: lending (part of your money in T-bills). Left of M.
  • y = 1: hold exactly M.
  • y > 1: borrowing at rf to buy more M (leverage). Right of M. Dollars borrowed = (y − 1) × wealth.

The separation property: the risky portfolio (M) is the same for everyone. Only y differs, and more risk-averse investors pick a smaller y.

3

Only efficient portfolios are on the CML

Individual stocks and inefficient portfolios plot below the CML. They carry firm-specific risk, which raises σ without raising expected return because nobody is compensated for it. That's why the CML can't price an individual stock. You need the SML, which uses β.

Lecture quiz“Asset A plots on the CML. ρA,M = +1.” Always. Anything on the CML is a mix of rf and M, so it moves in lockstep with M.
4

The Puzzle: total vs. systematic risk

In the lecture figure, M, A, B and X all have the same E(rM) but sit further and further right (more σ). All of them are correctly priced. How can more σ earn no more return? Because the extra σ is firm-specific, and the market doesn't pay for it. Same E(r) means same β means same systematic risk.

Which is riskier?Total risk (σ)Systematic risk (β)
M or X?XSame (β = 1)
V or Y?YSame (β = 0.5)
M or V?Same σM
W, X, Y or Z?Same σW > X > Y > Z
F (risk-free) or Z?ZSame (β = 0)

Dr. Dye crossed out the total-risk column on purpose. Only systematic risk determines expected return. Read E(r) off the vertical axis and you know β: points at the same height have the same β.

Sessions 14–16 · Capital Asset Pricing Model

CAPM and the Security Market Line

Required return depends only on systematic risk. Once you know what an asset's return should be, you know what its price should be.

You're ready when you can…

Learn

1

Where β comes from

Apply the row trick to the market portfolio:

σM2 = w1Cov(r1, rM) + w2Cov(r2, rM) + … + wnCov(rn, rM)

Hold asset i alone and your risk is σi2. Hold it inside the market and your exposure is only Cov(ri, rM), its systematic risk. Divide both sides by σM2:

βi ≡ Cov(ri, rM)σM2 = ρi,MσiσM  ⟹  1 = w1β1 + … + wnβn = βM

So β is an asset's systematic risk relative to the market's (“SYSi / SYSM”). βM = 1 and βrf = 0.

2

Why only systematic risk is priced

Under the CML, every investor holds the market, so nobody is exposed to any asset's firm-specific risk. And because everyone holds the same portfolio, everyone agrees on each asset's risk: Cov(ri, rM). (Remember the car dealer and the dentist who bought GM: they disagree on its risk only because they hold different portfolios.)

From the lecture notes“Total risk plays no part whatsoever because no one is exposed to total risk since everyone is diversified (holds the market).”
3

The Security Market Line

In equilibrium, every asset pays the same reward per unit of systematic risk as the market:

E(ri) − rfCov(ri, rM) = E(rM) − rfCov(rM, rM)  ⟹  SML: ki = rf + βi[E(rM) − rf]

Read it as y = b + mx: the intercept is rf, the slope is the market risk premium, and the x-axis is β, not σ. Portfolio β is a simple weighted average, βp = Σ wiβi. For example, 60% in the market and 40% in T-bills gives β = 0.6(1) + 0.4(0) = 0.6.

Trapβp is a weighted average. σp2 is never Σ wiσi2; it's the double summation.
4

Same notation, different meanings: E(r) vs. k

Security analysis: expected returnCAPM: required return k
Uses today's price and forecast cash flowsUses β and current market conditions
E(r) = [E(P1) + E(Div1) − P0] / P0k = rf + β[E(rM) − rf]
A forecast: what we think the return will beNot a forecast: what the return should be

Given the cash flows, price and expected return move in opposite directions. The correct price V0 is the price that makes E(r) = k:

P0 = E(CF1)1 + E(r)    V0 = E(CF1)1 + k    α ≡ E(r) − k    NPV = −P0 + V0

Lecture example: analysts expect Stock Z to pay $12 and trade at $450 next year, so E(CF1) = $462. If CAPM says k = 12.5%, then V0 = 462 / 1.125 = $412.50. At P0 = $440, E(r) = 5%, which is too low, so the price is too high.

5

β from a covariance matrix

When you're handed a covariance matrix and market weights, use the row trick. For a two-asset market:

Cov(r1, rM) = w1σ12 + w2Cov(r1, r2)    σM2 = w1Cov(r1, rM) + w2Cov(r2, rM)

Then β1 = Cov(r1, rM)/σM2. Check your work: w1β1 + w2β2 must equal 1.

6

Solving backwards on the SML

Two correctly priced assets pin down the line. Slope: MRP = [E(r1) − E(r2)] / (β1 − β2). Intercept: rf = E(r1) − β1·MRP. To hit a target β with two assets: wA = (βtarget − βB)/(βA − βB).

7

Assumptions and implications

Assumptions
  1. Investors are price takers (perfect competition).
  2. One identical holding period.
  3. Same set of assets for all, including risk-free borrowing and lending.
  4. No taxes or transaction costs.
  5. Mean-variance optimizers (everyone looks for the efficient frontier).
  6. Homogeneous expectations: identical E(r), σ and ρ estimates.
Implications for equilibrium
  1. Identical risky portfolios for all investors (follows from 2, 3, 5, 6).
  2. Everyone holds the market portfolio.
  3. The market portfolio is value-weighted and includes all traded assets.
  4. The market is the tangency portfolio; complete portfolios lie on the CML.
  5. No investor is exposed to unique risk.
  6. Risk premiums depend on systematic risk only.
  7. The market risk premium rises with σM2 and with aggregate risk aversion.

Sessions 17–19 · Single Index Model (SIM)

The Single Index Model

Regress a stock's excess return on the market's. The slope is β, the intercept is α, and the leftovers are firm-specific risk.

You're ready when you can…

Learn

1

Factor models

A factor model explains stock returns by their relationship to a systematic factor f (interest rates, GDP, inflation…). It lets you estimate E(r), variances and covariances, and partition total risk into factor and non-factor parts.

ri,t = ai + bift + ei,t    (actual = predicted by factor + residual)

Assumptions: (1) exactly one systematic factor explains returns; (2) returns are linearly related to that factor; (3) residuals are random, with E(ei) = 0 and Cov(ei, ej) = 0 for i ≠ j.

Lecture example: with ai = 0.04 and bi = 1.26, period 26 had r = 11.2% and f = 2.5%. Predicted = 0.04 + 1.26(0.025) = 0.0715, so the residual e = 0.112 − 0.0715 = 0.0405.

2

The SIM uses the market and excess returns

The SIM picks a market index (like the S&P 500) as the factor and uses excess returns: Ri = ri − rf and RM = rM − rf. The parameters are renamed α and β.

Generic single factorSingle Index Model
ri,t = ai + bift + ei,tRi,t = αi + βiRM,t + ei,t
E(ri) = ai + biE(f)E(Ri) = αi + βiE(RM)
σi2 = bi2σf2 + σei2σi2 = βi2σM2 + σei2
Cov(ri, rj) = bibjσf2 + Cov(ei, ej)Cov(Ri, Rj) = βiβjσM2 + Cov(ei, ej)
R-SQR = bi2σf2/σi2R-SQR = βi2σM2/σi2

Link to CAPM: CAPM says E(ri) − rf = βi[E(rM) − rf], which is E(Ri) = βiE(RM). That's the SIM with α = 0. If CAPM is correct, R̄i = βiR̄M, so a nonzero α is ex-post evidence of mispricing.

3

The line of best fit

We want residuals as small as possible. Their plain sum always cancels to zero, so OLS minimizes the sum of squared errors (SSE). The best-fit line (1) passes through (R̄M, R̄i) and (2) has slope Cov/Var:

βi = Cov(Ri, RM)σM2    αi = R̄i − βiR̄M    σei2 = 1n − 1Σ ei,t2 = SSEn − 1

Because Σ et = 0, the mean residual is zero. Two stat facts get used: Var(c) = 0 and Var(cx) = c2Var(x).

4

Partitioning risk (ANOVA)

Var(Ri) = Var(βiRM) + Var(ei), so:

σi2  =  βi2σM2  +  σei2   ⟺   SSTn − 1 = SS Regn − 1 + SSEn − 1total = systematic + firm-specific (unique)

Systematic risk depends on how volatile the market is (σM2) and how sensitive the stock is to it (β). R-square is systematic risk as a share of total risk, and it equals the squared correlation:

R2 = βi2σM2σi2 = SS RegSST = ρi,M2   (β = ρσi/σM ⟹ β2σM2 = ρ2σi2)

7

Covariances, expected returns and portfolios

Residuals are assumed uncorrelated, so Cov(ei, ej) = 0 and Cov(Ri, Rj) = βiβjσM2. Lecture: Cov(GOOG, GS) = 1.16478 × 1.62593 × 0.001236 = 0.002340. Correlation follows as ρij = Cov/(σiσj) = ρiMρjM.

Expected total return: E(ri) = rf + αi + βi[E(rM) − rf].

For portfolios, βp = Σ wiβi, αp = Σ wiαi, and residual variance σep2 = Σ wi2σei2. That last one shrinks toward zero as you diversify. So σp2 = βp2σM2 + σep2.

Why bother? For n stocks, the SIM needs n α's + n β's + n σe's + E(RM) + σM2 = 3n + 2 estimates, instead of n(n − 1)/2 separate covariances.

Flashcards

When to use what

Top exam traps

  • CML uses σ and holds only efficient portfolios. SML uses β and prices every asset. Never put a single stock's σ into the CML to get its required return.
  • α = forecast E(r) − required k. Positive α means the stock is above the SML: underpriced, so buy.
  • Same β means same required return, whatever the σ. Higher σ at the same β is just more firm-specific risk.
  • Only M has both β = 1 and ρ = +1. Another asset with β = 1 usually has ρ < 1.
  • The SIM regression uses excess returns. Convert back to total returns by adding rf.
  • Variances use n − 1 on this formula sheet, including σe2 = SSE/(n − 1).
  • βp and αp are weighted averages. σp never is (unless ρ = 1).
  • Under SIM, Cov(Ri, Rj) = βiβjσM2. Don't add residual covariance unless you're told it's nonzero.
  • R2 = ρ2, so ρ = ±√R2 with the same sign as β.
